How the lines are drawn

A related-party transaction is business between a company and one of its own insiders: a director, an executive, a 5% shareholder, or their family. Most are ordinary. The rules ask three questions in turn, and only the last two decide whether a deal is material, meaning big enough that a director can no longer be counted as independent.

Three questions

1 · SEC ITEM 404 Must the company tell shareholders? > $120,000 Any deal with an insider above $120,000 a year is listed in the proxy statement. Being listed is not a red flag. It's just transparency. e.g. an executive's son-in-law on the payroll at $150,000
→
2 · ISS, ADVICE Is a director's firm giving the company advice? > $10,000 Legal, consulting, banking, audit, insurance or marketing advice is held to a very low bar: advisers can sway decisions. Above $10,000 a year, the director isn't independent. applies to outside directors only
→
3 · ISS / NYSE / NASDAQ, SIZE Is any other deal big for whoever gets paid? % of revenue The deal is compared with the annual revenue of the party receiving the money. NYSE: over the greater of $1M or 2%. Nasdaq: over the greater of $200K or 5%. $2.3B is huge, but only 3.6% of Broadcom's $63.9B

The size line grows with the recipient

Below a certain size, the fixed floor ($1M NYSE, $200K Nasdaq) sets the line. Above it, the percentage takes over. Nasdaq's 5% is looser than NYSE's 2% for big recipients, so the same deal can pass on one exchange and fail on the other. Hover the chart.

Not tested, and why

  • 🔒 Needs private data: if the money goes to a private company, its revenue isn't public, so the line can't be computed. Only the board and that company know. This is a disclosure limit, not a research gap.
  • Own pay: an executive's salary or stock isn't a related-party deal. SEC Item 404 leaves it to the pay tables.
  • Former insiders: ISS asks what a director currently has, so a past director's deal is listed but not tested.
